# Methodology: conducting interviews and discovery

## Conducting Interviews and Discovery

Discovery is the initial phase that seeks a deep understanding of the current context, the problems and the opportunities an AI solution can address. It is often done through interviews and process observation. The effectiveness of this stage defines the quality of the next ones, since a good understanding of the problem is the basis of a successful solution.

## Prior Preparation

Careful preparation boosts interview results:

- **Context research:** study the sector, the organization (or the area in question) and any materials provided (e.g., process documents, reports, org charts) in advance to understand terms, work dynamics and general context.
- **Clear objectives:** define what you want to learn in each interview and prepare key questions aligned with these objectives. For example, if the focus is a purchasing process, identify its steps and possible difficulties to guide your questions.
- **Flexible script:** have a script of topics, but be ready to adapt it as the conversation evolves. The best interviews flow naturally; what matters is making sure all critical points are covered by the end.

## Types of Effective Questions

Combining different types of questions helps extract richer information:

- **Open questions:** encourage broad, in-depth descriptions (e.g., *"Tell me how this process works today."*).
- **Operational questions:** focus on how things are done (e.g., *"Which tools do you use to record an order?"*).
- **Exception questions:** explore unusual situations or problems (e.g., *"What happens when an urgent order arrives outside business hours?"*).
- **Impact questions:** investigate consequences and importance (e.g., *"What happens if this activity is delayed or fails?"*).
- **Clarification questions:** confirm understanding (e.g., *"So first X happens and then Y, correct?"*).

## Interview Conduct and Structure

A productive interview usually follows these stages:

1. **Opening:** introduce yourself and briefly explain the purpose of the conversation, building empathy and a relaxed atmosphere. Make it clear you are there to understand and help improve, not to evaluate people.
2. **Initial exploration:** let the interviewee freely describe the process or problem in general terms. Practice active listening, giving them room to talk.
3. **Deep dive:** as the account progresses, use specific questions to explore the details of each step, variation or difficulty mentioned. Look for concrete examples and quantities (even approximate).
4. **Constructive challenge:** test the consistency of some statements by asking for clarification or supporting data (e.g., *"Can you show me a typical report you prepare?"*). This helps separate perceptions from facts.
5. **Recap and closing:** in the final minutes, summarize what you understood and ask for confirmation. Correct any misunderstandings and thank them for their collaboration, explaining how the information will be used from now on.

## Building Rapport and Trust

Conduct the interviews in a way that builds trust and openness:

- Start with small informal talk to build closeness before getting into the formal topics.
- Show empathy and respect: listen without interrupting, avoid negative reactions or judgments. Use verbal agreement and body language that show attention.
- Ensure confidentiality and safety: reinforce that any information will be used only to improve processes, not to evaluate people. Make sure the environment is safe for sharing problems.
- If needed, share analogous cases (in a generic way) to show that similar difficulties have already been identified and solved in other contexts. This can encourage the interviewee to open up.

## Who to Interview

To get a complete picture, look for complementary perspectives:

- **Operational executors:** people who perform the tasks under analysis every day. They describe the real process, including informal deviations and the difficulties they face.
- **Managers or area owners:** provide the macro view, aligning possible cases with strategic priorities and pointing out constraints or available resources.
- **End users or customers of the process (if applicable):** when the process serves other areas or external customers, their perceptions of quality and pain points are also relevant.

When planning the interviews, combine representatives from several levels. For example, if the case involves a sales process, listen to both salespeople and sales leaders and, if possible, an impacted customer or end user.

## Signs of Successful vs. Ineffective Interviews

After the interviews, reflect on the result of each one. Look for signs of success or problems in the information gathering:

| Successful interview | Unproductive interview |
|---|---|
| Open, trusting atmosphere: the interviewee shares information freely, without fear. | Tense or evasive atmosphere: short, cautious answers, little spontaneity. |
| Answers rich in concrete details (examples, approximate numbers, names of systems or documents). | Vague, generic answers without clear examples; hard to understand the process or problem. |
| Valuable insights emerge: information that not even those involved had articulated before, or the real causes of problems are revealed. | No really new information; only repetition of the obvious or of what was already known. |
| Effective time management: all relevant topics were covered without losing focus. | Frequent deviations from the main subject; not enough time to cover critical agenda points. |

## Running Several Interviews

In complex scenarios, it is common to run multiple interviews with different people. Some tips for handling several interviews:

- **Immediate documentation:** after each meeting, record the main points and facts in a standardized format (for example: categories of current process, problems mentioned, improvement suggestions, etc.). This makes it easier to consolidate data later and compare accounts.
- **Comparative synthesis:** when the interviews are done, put the information side by side. Evaluate where the interviewees agree and where they diverge. Divergences may indicate misunderstandings or important process variations that deserve investigation.
- **Supporting technology:** if possible, record or transcribe the conversations (with the participants' permission). This lets you return to exact details and avoids losing information.
- **Breaks between sessions:** plan short breaks between interviews to review notes and refresh your memory. This helps you start each next conversation prepared and reduces mental fatigue.

## Internal Language and Jargon

Every organization or team has its own vocabulary. Write down the particular terms and expressions mentioned during the interviews:

- **Ask for explanations of acronyms and specific terms:** never pretend to understand jargon you do not know. This does not reduce your credibility; on the contrary, it shows interest in fully understanding the context.
- **Build a mini glossary:** keep a record of the jargon and its meaning. In the documentation and in building the solution, use the local terminology whenever appropriate (for example, in prompts or interfaces), but explain its meaning so that an outsider can also understand the document.

## Challenging Behaviors in Interviews

Dealing with different personalities is part of discovery:

- **Resistant interviewee:** be patient and focus on positive results. Reiterate that the goal is to make work easier, not to monitor or expose anyone. Sometimes sharing a generic AI success story can reduce resistance.
- **Evasive interviewee:** if the answers are too generic or vague, try to redirect with more direct questions, or present a hypothetical scenario to clarify a specific point. Explain the reason for the questions to motivate more precise answers.
- **Talkative or dominant interviewee:** value their expertise but keep control of the time. Use phrases like *"These are great points; shall we go back to step X of the process to understand it better?"* to redirect the conversation.
